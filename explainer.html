The Hammer,
Explained

And the mirror it hangs: a short course in striking, prying and holding things up.

I
Anatomy

What is a hammer, exactly?

Two parts and a wedge. Every curve on it has a job.

Two parts and a wedge

A hammer is a steel head and a handle, locked together by a small wedge. Keep scrolling and it assembles.

The face

The striking surface. Many faces are slightly domed (belled), which forgives an off-center hit and lets you set a nail flush without leaving a crescent-shaped dent.

Neck and cheeks

The neck carries the face out from the body. The flat cheeks on either side are the mass behind the blow.

Eye and wedge

The eye is the hole the handle passes through. A wedge driven into the top of the handle spreads the wood so the head can't fly off. A loose head is the first thing to check on an old hammer.

The claw

Two curved prongs with a V-shaped slot that slips under a nail head. With the head as a pivot, the whole hammer becomes a lever (see Chapter IV).

Handle, throat and swell

Length gives you speed and leverage. The narrow throat just under the head is where handles usually break after a missed blow; the flared swell at the end keeps the handle from sliding out of your hand.

Field note Hammer faces are hardened. Never strike two hammers together: hardened steel can chip and throw sharp fragments.
II
The physics of a blow

Why can a lighter hammer hit as hard?

Because speed counts twice.

The energy a hammer delivers is ½ × mass × speed². Mass counts once; speed counts twice. Double the weight and you double the energy. Double the speed and you get four times the energy.

That's why a light hammer swung briskly can match a heavier one swung slowly, and why heavy hammers are about momentum and fatigue as much as force. Drag the sliders and watch the curve.

2×speed=4×energy

III
Faces and marks

What does each face leave behind?

Every hammer signs its work.

A milled face is cross-hatched like a waffle, so it bites the nail head and won't glance off. It's great for framing and terrible for anything you'll see. A dead-blow mallet is hollow and partly filled with loose shot. On impact the shot arrives a split-second late, which smothers the bounce.

IV
The claw is a lever

How does a claw out-pull your arm?

The head is the pivot; the handle is the long arm.

When you pull a nail, the hammer's head rocks on the wood. That contact point is the pivot. The nail sits in the claw about an inch from it; your hand is a foot away. That difference in distance is the leverage.

Hand force = nail's grip × (claw distance ÷ handle length). A 13-inch handle against a claw about 1¼ inches out gives roughly 10 to 1. Draft: we'll measure our hammers.

Try it
Field note Slide a scrap block under the head. It raises the pivot for a straighter pull and keeps the head from denting your wall.

Intermission · The Swap Meet

What to check on a vintage hammer

  1. Wiggle the head. Any play means the wedge has loosened.
  2. Look at the face edge. Chips or a curled "mushroomed" rim mean it's been abused.
  3. Sight down the handle. Cracks near the throat are where handles fail.
  4. Check the claw tips. Bent or chipped prongs won't grip a nail head.

VI
Where hammer meets mirror

How much does a mirror really pull on the wall?

More than it weighs, if the wire is flat.

A mirror hung on wire splits its weight between two sides of a V. The flatter the V, the harder each side must pull to hold the same weight up: tension = weight ÷ (2 × sin of the angle).

At a 30° angle each side carries exactly the mirror's weight. At 10°, a 30 lb mirror puts about 86 lb on each side. Choose wire and hooks for the tension, not the weight.
